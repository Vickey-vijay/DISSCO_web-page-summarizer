This page explains how Fresnel lenses and rotating mechanisms allow lighthouses to project focused, flashing beams of light that guide ships safely at sea.

- In 1822, French engineer Augustin Fresnel built a lens using many thin glass rings and prisms arranged in a circle to bend lamp light into a flat, narrow beam.
- Large lenses turn slowly on a bed of liquid mercury or on ball bearings, driven by a clockwork motor, making the beams sweep across the sea like the spokes of a wheel.

## L05: Wikipedia: Fresnel lens

```text
This page provides an overview of the history, development, design, and diverse modern applications of the Fresnel lens.
- Invented for lighthouses by Augustin-Jean Fresnel, the first prototype was finished in March 1820 with François Soleil and François Arago, and was officially lit at the Cordouan Lighthouse on July 25, 1823.
- Augustin Fresnel became Secretary of the Commission des Phares in May 1824 and died at age 39 on July 14, 1827, after establishing standard lighthouse lens sizes and catadioptric prism designs.
- In 1852, John L. Gilliland patented a method for making one-piece stepped dioptric lenses from pressed glass, and John Hopkinson invented the group-flashing lens in 1884.
- Fresnel lenses reduce material thickness and mass by using concentric annular sections, and were historically used in lighthouses, automobile headlamps, and theater lighting.
- Modern uses include solar power applications, traffic lights, virtual reality headsets like the Meta Quest 2, camera viewfinders, overhead projectors, and passive infrared motion detectors.
```
